Keeping Your Corona Water Heater Running Longer

Routine maintenance makes all the difference between a water heater that runs for years and one that floods the garage in year seven. Our advice for homeowners is a short list: a yearly flush, an anode inspection every few years, a yearly test of the T&P valve, a temperature setting around 120 degrees, and annual descaling for tankless units on hard water, so small issues get caught early.

The age of your heater matters just as much. With regular maintenance, a tank water heater usually runs 8 to 12 years, a heat pump water heater 10 to 15, and a tankless unit up to 20. Once a heater passes that range and starts showing warning signs, like discolored hot water, rumbling, or a damp spot under the tank, it's time to plan a replacement. Replacing on your own schedule costs less and causes far less stress than scrambling for a new heater after a leak.

The earlier you report a hot water problem, the cheaper it usually is. A tank that's just begun to rumble needs only a flush, yet that same buildup, ignored for a few years, can create hot spots on the tank floor and burn out the lower element on an electric model, turning a simple visit into a replacement. Save our number in your contacts today so help is one call away the moment something seems off. A quick call saves time, money, and cold showers.

Typical Water Heater Issues in Corona

Most water heater failures we see in Corona start with what's in the water. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, minerals can be a real factor: dissolved minerals settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, insulating the water from the burner, which produces the popping and rumbling you may hear during a heating cycle. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, and that's why regular descaling is worth keeping up with where the water is hard.

Equipment age plays a big role too. When the sacrificial anode rod wears out, often well before the tank itself looks old, the steel starts to rust from the inside, and a drip from the bottom seam is usually the first visible sign. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, which means we service both sides: thermocouples, burners, and venting on gas units, and heating elements and thermostats on electric ones.

Well water can bring sediment, iron, or sulfur into the tank, and a sulfur smell in the hot water usually points to a reaction with the anode rod that a different rod can fix. Older installations often need code updates when a heater is replaced: an expansion tank where a check valve or pressure regulator creates a closed system, a properly routed T&P discharge line, drain pans where a leak would hit finished floors, and seismic straps where local code requires them. Every one of these problems is fixable, and most cost far less to address early. If something seems off, a diagnostic visit from our team can keep a small problem from turning into a flooded floor.
